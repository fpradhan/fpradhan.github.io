## What the exemplars do

Read from the four PDFs. Both pages are laid out the same way, and both reflections follow the
same six beats.

| | Crow | Greiner |
|---|---|---|
| Objective statement at the top | yes, labelled *Objective:* | yes, unlabelled |
| Artifacts | run sequentially down the page | behind *Artifact 1 / Artifact 2* tabs |
| Course line | `Course: HPTT 802 – Instructional Design for Health Professions Education` | named in the first sentence instead |
| Link to the artifact | at the end | a button, labelled with the assignment name |
| Reflection length | 330–400 words | 230–250 words |

The reflection anatomy is consistent enough to treat as a formula: name the artifact and its
course, state the purpose of the assignment, say why it was chosen against this objective, say
what changed, name the challenge outright, then land on a current or future use. Greiner signposts
it almost mechanically — *"The purpose of this assignment was to…"*, *"I chose this assignment
because…"*, *"The challenge I encountered with this assignment was…"*. That is worth copying,
because it makes each of the six required elements findable by whoever is grading.

Two departures worth making deliberately.

**Length.** Crow runs 330 to 400 words, over the stated 200–300. The drafts here sit at 243–270,
nearer Greiner, and inside the limit.

**Prose.** Crow's writing carries several of the exact patterns the AI-writing guidance says to
avoid: *"It serves as a foundation I will revisit and refine"*, *"This reflection captures my
commitment to ongoing growth and evidence-based instruction"*, *"marks a key turning point"*.
Those are copula replacement, a promotional closing, and a phrase from the undue-significance
list. Copy the structure and the completeness; do not copy the register. The drafts below were
checked against that list and came back clean.

## Draft reflections

Each is 200–300 words and covers the six required elements. Read them as first drafts in your
voice, not finished text — the assumptions I had to make are listed after each one.

### 1-A — Curriculum Outline

**Course:** HPTT 801 – Foundations of Health Professions Education (Fall 2023)
{: .meta}

HPTT 801, Foundations of Health Professions Education, was the first course I took in this
program, in the fall of 2023. The Curriculum Outline assignment asked for a full course rather
than a single teaching session: outcomes, a week-by-week sequence, and an assessment plan that
matched them. I wrote a fifteen-week medical student course on the pathophysiology of the liver,
built around five outcomes, with weekly multiple choice questions, six case discussions, and a
final care plan the students complete in the electronic medical record.

I selected this artifact for Instructional Methods because it is the only piece of coursework in
which I developed an entire program rather than one component of one. Currently, most of my
teaching happens at the bedside and is shaped by whichever patients are on the service that week.
Building a course from nothing required me to treat sequencing, signaling what matters, and
cutting redundancy as deliberate decisions rather than instincts. I found deciding what to leave
out harder than deciding what to include.

What I learned became clearer later, when I revisited this course for a subsequent assignment. I
had named one theoretical framework for the weekly open-book assessment, and on rereading it I
felt the format was better explained by the zone of proximal development. A closed-book
examination would have been more consistent with what I had written. I kept the open-book format,
because trainee stress mattered more to me than internal consistency, and I would make the same
call again.

I have since applied this to the Enhanced Medical Education Track, the four-year supplemental
curriculum I established with the Department of Surgery.

### 1-B — Clinical Teaching Project

**Course:** HPTT 840 – Foundations of Clinical Teaching (Summer 2026)
{: .meta}

HPTT 840, Foundations of Clinical Teaching, ran in the summer of 2026. The purpose of the Clinical
Teaching Project was to take one procedure we already teach clinically and build a complete
instructional plan around it, a component at a time across the term, with every choice justified
against published evidence rather than habit. It was assembled in Week 12 from five parts:
learning objectives, a teaching strategy, a reflection scaffold, a clinical reasoning analysis,
and an assessment, with a procedural checklist appended. Mine covers simulation-based teaching of
diagnostic paracentesis on a task trainer.

I selected this artifact for Instructional Methods because every component had to be justified
against published evidence rather than preference. I wrote three objectives in ABCD format across
the cognitive, psychomotor and affective domains, chose Peyton's Four-Step Approach for the
psychomotor objective, and used the validated REFLECT rubric as the reflection scaffold rather
than asking students to reflect freely.

The affective objective was the hardest part and taught me the most. I wrote an objective
requiring the student to raise a guideline-based concern with the supervising physician, because
the willingness to speak up on a patient's behalf is not something a procedural checklist produces
on its own. Writing the objective was straightforward. Working out how to score it was not.

In paracentesis and in endoscopy I cannot let trainees learn through consequence, because a
mishandled needle or a mishandled loop has a cost the patient carries. Simulation is where that
learning has to happen instead, and until this project I had no defensible structure for it. I
plan to use the checklist and the rubric with the students entering the Enhanced Medical Education
Track.

### 2-A — e-Module, *Introduction to Steatotic Liver Disease*

**Course:** HPTT 825 – Essentials of E-Module Development (Summer 2024)
{: .meta}

HPTT 825, Essentials of E-Module Development, ran in the summer of 2024. The final project asked
us to build a working e-learning module in Articulate Storyline, developed across the term from a
needs analysis and a slide-by-slide storyboard. Mine is aimed at medical students: three
objectives, a patient case as the opening hook, and assessment interspersed through the module
rather than collected at the end.

I selected this artifact for Innovation in Teaching because it is the most interactive thing I
have built. Currently, students meet steatotic liver disease in a lecture, if they meet it at all.
The needs analysis I wrote for the module cited both the prevalence data and the gap in hepatology
teaching in undergraduate medical education.

What I did not expect was that writing the feedback would be harder than writing the questions.
For each item I wrote separate correct, incorrect and hint responses, and the incorrect ones are
where the teaching actually happens. A wrong answer on a liver enzyme pattern needed to explain
why that pattern points somewhere else, not simply record that the answer was wrong.

The interdisciplinary element came through peer review. I completed structured reviews of
classmates' modules using the Nebraska E-Learning Scorecard, one on microscopy in dentistry and
one on EKG interpretation. Applying the criteria to fields I know nothing about made them clearer
to me than applying them to my own module had.

The challenge I have not solved is distribution. A Storyline file cannot be opened without the
software, so a module only reaches learners if somebody hosts it. When I built a second module the
following year, on portal hypertension, I published it through the UNMC E-Gallery for that reason.

### 2-B — Simulation Scenario Builder

**Course:** HPTT 824 – Simulated and Virtual Learning in Health Professions Education (Fall 2025)
{: .meta}

HPTT 824, Simulated and Virtual Learning in Health Professions Education, ran in the fall of 2025.
The purpose of the Simulation Scenario Builder was to design a simulation that could actually be
run, rather than describe one in the abstract. It is an eighteen-sheet workbook completed in
stages and submitted in full in Week 16, covering needs assessment, objectives, fidelity
decisions, the scenario algorithm itself, equipment, participant roles, and a pilot test plan.

I selected this artifact for Innovation in Teaching because a simulation scenario cannot be built
alone. The workbook required me to assign roles, specify equipment, and write what each
participant does at each stage of the case. That is the collaborative and interdisciplinary half
of the objective made concrete rather than asserted.

The scenario algorithm taught me the most. I had to write the vital sign states the manikin moves
through as the case progresses to a vagal response and then to pulseless electrical activity, and
specify what triggers each transition. Writing branches for what learners might do, rather than a
script for what I hoped they would do, was a different kind of planning than I had done before.

The fidelity decisions were where I struggled. Higher fidelity is not automatically better, and I
found it difficult to judge how much realism a given objective actually needed. In an earlier
assignment in this same course I placed myself at advanced beginner on Benner's scale for
simulation, and that still feels accurate.

Our fellowship does not run procedural simulation in any structured way. This workbook is the
format I would use to propose one.
